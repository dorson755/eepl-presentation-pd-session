Slide 1 Title — AI in Education: Closer Than People Think

Advance from the gallery into the deck.

Open with a practical tone:

"Today is not about making AI sound impressive. It is about making it usable."

Set the central claim: AI is already within reach for ordinary teachers. It is not distant, elitist, or reserved for technical insiders. The audience should understand immediately that this talk is grounded in real classroom usefulness.

Slide 2 Why AI Still Feels Distant

Reveal each perception card one at a time.

Validate the room's hesitation. Acknowledge that AI often sounds too technical, feels like a specialist tool, arrives with hype instead of help, and is sometimes discussed like a private club.

Key line:

"The intimidation around AI is real, but much of it is created by the way AI is discussed rather than by the actual difficulty of starting to use it."

Slide 3 (the other) Accessibility

Reveal the three accessibility statements, ending with the positive framing.

This is the key framing slide. Correct the meaning of accessibility: not only accessibility for learners, but accessibility as in-reach — how easy AI is to begin using, how available it already is, and how little status or technical prestige is required.

Strong line:

"AI is one of the few powerful tools that does not first ask who you know — it asks what you want help with."

Pause on this line. The audience can still react privately from their phones, but do not call attention to it.

Slide 4 When Tech Sounds Deeper Than It Is

Reveal each row of the comparison table.

Expose the performance of fake depth. If a sentence about AI sounds deep but does not help a teacher plan a lesson, assess understanding, or teach more clearly, it may just be jargon pretending to be wisdom.

Encourage the audience to laugh and relax. The contrast is between impressive-sounding language and a simple Monday-morning action.

Slide 5 AI or Human?
Interactive Guessing Game
  1. Show two lesson plans side by side.
  2. Ask the audience to vote on which one was written by AI.
  3. Reveal the answer and discuss what gave it away — or what made it convincing.
  4. Run a second round if the room is enjoying it.

The point: clear prompting can produce classroom work that sounds surprisingly human and useful. The game also reinforces that the ask matters more than the tool.

Slide 6 The Good, the Bad, and the Ugly

Reveal each column one at a time.

Give a balanced reality check. AI can sound human and be useful, but it also carries real risks. Frame the conversation around three categories:

  • The Good: saves time, generates ideas, differentiates materials, and lowers barriers.
  • The Bad: hallucinations, bias, privacy concerns, and the risk of over-reliance.
  • The Ugly: cheating, deepfakes, job-loss anxiety, and ethical misuse in high-stakes decisions.

Key line:

"The goal is not to ignore the risks. It is to keep the good, manage the bad, and prevent the ugly."

Slide 7 AI Is Already in Teachers' Hands

Reveal the three use-case cards, then the RAND statistic.

Move from theory to proof. Teachers are already using AI for lesson planning, classroom materials, and communication. RAND reported that roughly one-quarter of surveyed U.S. teachers used AI tools for instructional planning or teaching in the 2023–2024 school year.

The point is not that every teacher is fully using AI. The point is that this has moved beyond theory into real workflows.

Slide 8 What AI Actually Gives Teachers

Reveal the Assistant, Partner, and Tutor cards.

Translate AI into teacher value. AI behaves most usefully as a fast support tool, not a magical replacement. Mention the OECD framing of generative AI as a tutor, partner, and assistant in education.

  • Assistant: drafts materials, creates rubrics and quizzes, produces summaries and examples.
  • Partner: brainstorms activities, suggests alternate explanations, supports differentiation.
  • Tutor: generates practice questions, offers multiple explanations, helps create review prompts.

Slide 10 Where Do I Actually Go to Use AI?

Reveal the three tool category cards, then the closing message.

Reassure the audience that they do not need to learn every tool. The prompting skill transfers across platforms. Recommend starting with whatever is already available or approved in their school.

  • General chat assistants: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot — good for drafting, rewriting, brainstorming, and practice questions.
  • Teaching-focused tools: MagicSchool, Eduaide, Diffit, Curipod — built-in templates, differentiation, rubrics, and standards alignment.
  • Tools already in the workflow: Google Docs, Microsoft Word, Canva — built-in AI writing help, slide makers, and grammar checks with no new login.

Key line:

"The tool matters less than the ask. A clear prompt in a basic chat assistant will usually beat a vague prompt in a fancy teaching tool."

Slide 11 From Lesson to Experience

Reveal the amplification ideas and the mini prompt example.

Show that AI can amplify and gamify lessons, not just save time. One solid prompt can become a quiz, a game, a roleplay, a revision task, and a differentiated resource set.

Mini prompt example to share:

"Turn this Grade 5 history topic into a 15-minute classroom quest with teams, clues, points, and a final reflection."

Slide 12 AI Can Also Improve Learner Access

Reveal each accessibility-for-learners card.

Bring back the other meaning of accessibility. AI can help teachers simplify explanations, adjust reading level, offer alternative examples, support varied language needs, and create multiple paths into the same concept.

Emphasize that this must be done carefully and ethically. AI supports inclusion when the teacher remains in control.

Slide 13 Use It, But Use It Wisely

Reveal the guardrail points in two columns.

Keep the presentation balanced and credible. AI is powerful and practical, but not beyond scrutiny. Practical AI use and responsible AI use belong together.

Live Poll — Which Safeguard Matters Most?
  1. Ask the audience: "Which guardrail is your top priority when using AI with students?"
  2. Options: Verify outputs, Protect student privacy, Keep the teacher in control, Do not outsource judgment.
  3. Use the result to emphasize that practical AI use and responsible AI use belong together.

Reference UNESCO guidance on human-centred use, ethical safeguards, and the protection of learners and educators. Key reminders: verify outputs, protect student privacy, keep the teacher in control, do not outsource judgment, and use AI to assist rather than replace thinking.

Slide 14 The Best Time to Start Is Small

Reveal the closing cards and final message.

End with a practical and empowering close. Teachers do not need to become AI experts overnight. They only need to begin using AI for one real classroom need at a time.

Preserve these closing lines:

"The goal is not to become an AI expert overnight. The goal is to become a better-equipped teacher."

"AI is not reserved for the connected, the technical, or the intimidating. In education, it becomes powerful in the hands of ordinary teachers doing practical work well."
