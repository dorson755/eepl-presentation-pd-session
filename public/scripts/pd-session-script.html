[Slide 1 — Title Slide]

Good morning everyone.

Protocol having been established, let me first express my sincere gratitude for the opportunity to be here with you today. It is truly an honour to stand before a room filled with educators who have chosen one of the most important professions in our country. You are the people who lay the foundation upon which every child’s future is built.

My name is Darnell Lightbourne, and I serve as an Executive Officer within the Government of The Bahamas, currently posted as the Library Supervisor at Elizabeth Estates Public Library. It is a privilege to join you for this Professional Development Session as we reflect on the theme, Building Strong Foundations: Literacy as the Heart of Early Learning.

This morning, I want us to think of literacy not simply as reading words on a page, but as the heart of communication, imagination, confidence, and lifelong learning. I also want us to consider the vital role teachers, families, libraries, and even technology can play in nurturing that literacy from the very earliest years.

[Next slide]

[Slide 2 — Every Small Effort Matters]

There is a Caribbean proverb that says, “Every mickle mek a muckle.” Simply put, every little bit adds up to something great.

As preschool teachers, you may sometimes wonder whether reading one more story, singing one more nursery rhyme, asking one more question, or taking one more trip to the library really makes a difference. I am here today to assure you that it does.

Every story you read, every song you sing, every question you answer, every hug you give, and every child you encourage to pick up a book becomes another building block in that child’s future. Those small moments may seem ordinary in the day-to-day life of a classroom, but they are shaping minds, hearts, and habits that can last a lifetime.

[Next slide]

[Slide 3 — Literacy Is More Than Reading Words]

Many people believe that reading is simply about learning letters, recognizing words, and eventually reading sentences. But reading is so much more than that.

Literacy begins long before a child reads independently. It begins when a child listens to a voice, speaks their thoughts, responds to a story, turns a page, points to a picture, sings a rhyme, or makes marks on paper. That is why we must think of literacy as including listening, speaking, reading, and writing.

Reading develops the whole child. It develops language. It develops thinking. It develops imagination. It develops empathy. It develops confidence. And ultimately, it helps shape the adults our children will become.

When we read to children, we introduce them to words they may never hear in everyday conversation. We strengthen their vocabulary, improve their listening skills, and help them express themselves with confidence. When children hear stories, they begin asking questions: “What happens next?” “Why did the character do that?” “What would I have done?” Without even realizing it, they are learning to think critically, solve problems, and make connections.

Early literacy supports every subject. It helps children in mathematics, in science, in social and emotional learning, and in every area where language helps them make sense of the world.

[Next slide]

[Slide 4 — The Teacher’s Responsibility]

As preschool teachers, you have an incredible opportunity. Never underestimate your influence.

When you read a story with excitement, children become excited. When you ask questions, children begin thinking. When you laugh during a story, children discover that books bring joy. When you make reading part of every day, children begin believing that books belong in everyday life.

That is why your daily responsibilities matter so deeply. Reading aloud with expression, encouraging predictions, discussing pictures, asking questions, letting children retell stories in their own words, and using songs, rhymes, puppets, and storytelling are not small classroom techniques. They are powerful acts of literacy building.

Please continue reading aloud. Use different voices. Ask children to predict what happens next. Encourage them to describe the pictures. Allow them to tell the story in their own words. Sing nursery rhymes. Use puppets. Let stories come alive. Those are the moments when books move from being objects on a shelf to becoming part of a child’s everyday life.

[Next slide]

[Slide 5 — Expanding a Child’s World Through Books]

Reading also does something even more powerful. It teaches children about people. It teaches them about feelings. It teaches them about kindness. It teaches them that not everyone looks like them, lives like them, or experiences life the way they do.

And that reminds me of a beautiful experience I recently had with my own family. I was sitting around the table with my two granddaughters, who were six and seven years old, along with my youngest child, who is also seven. We were having a conversation about The Bahamas when my youngest granddaughter, Harmony, confidently said to my daughter, “Kayla, everybody born in the Bahamas.”

I smiled and gently replied, “Harmony, not everyone is born in The Bahamas. Some people are born in other countries.” She looked at me with complete surprise and said, “What? I didn’t know that!” I then said, “Yes, Harmony. Even Michaela is one of those people. She wasn’t born in The Bahamas.” Harmony immediately turned toward her aunt with wide eyes and exclaimed, “What?” Everyone around the table smiled.

But while everyone else was smiling, I was thinking. I realized that Harmony wasn’t being ignorant. She was simply making sense of the world based on what she knew. Her world was small — and that’s exactly how childhood begins. Children naturally believe that everyone experiences life the way they do until someone lovingly expands their world.

That moment reminded me why books are so important. Books allow children to travel without ever leaving their homes or classrooms. They introduce children to different countries, different cultures, different languages, different families, and different ways of living. Books help children understand that although we are different, we all belong to one human family.

[Next slide]

[Slide 6 — Partnership Between Home and School]

Children do not become readers only in school. They become readers at bedtime, on the sofa, around the dinner table, and during quiet moments with the people who love them most.

That is why the partnership between home and school matters so much. Literacy is strongest when it is reinforced across both environments. Teachers can encourage parents to read at bedtime, share simple discussion questions, send home books or literacy activities, invite family storytelling, and help parents understand that reading should feel joyful rather than stressful.

The goal is not to place another burden on already busy families. The goal is connection. A simple story, a conversation about pictures, a nursery rhyme, or a child retelling what happened in a book can all become meaningful literacy moments.

As librarians, we cannot do this work alone. Teachers cannot do it alone. Parents cannot do it alone. But together, we can create a generation of children who see reading not as a chore, but as an adventure.

[Next slide]

[Slide 7 — Preparing Children for a Digital World]

We also live in a world where children are growing up surrounded by technology. So as we build literacy, we must also help prepare them for a digital world.

Digital literacy should complement — not replace — books, conversation, play, and relationships. Technology can be useful when it is age-appropriate, purposeful, and guided by caring adults.

Teachers can introduce age-appropriate digital resources, pair digital stories with discussion and hands-on follow-up, teach safe and responsible use, and help children become thoughtful, creative users of technology. A digital story, for example, should still invite children to predict, respond, talk, and connect. The tool may be different, but the literacy habits remain the same.

So, this is not about banning technology. It is about using it intentionally and responsibly, in ways that support the whole child rather than replacing the human interaction children need most.

[Next slide]

[Slide 8 — The Preschool Library as a Literacy Hub]

That simple conversation around my dining table became a lesson for all of us. It reminded me that every child deserves opportunities to see beyond their own experiences. That is exactly what libraries provide.

A library is much more than a building filled with books. A library is a place where curiosity is welcomed. A place where questions are encouraged. A place where imagination is celebrated. A place where every child — regardless of their background — has the opportunity to discover something new.

A preschool library, in particular, can become a true literacy hub. It can be a welcoming space for curiosity and imagination, a place with child-friendly book displays, diverse and culturally relevant books, storytelling, read-alouds, puppets, and hands-on literacy activities. It can also be a bridge between the school and the wider community.

Sometimes people ask me, “Why should I bring my child to the library when everything is online?” My answer is simple: because libraries do something technology alone cannot do. Libraries create experiences. They create conversations. They build communities. A librarian doesn’t simply hand a child a book. A librarian helps that child discover books that speak to their interests, nurture their imagination, and encourage a lifelong love of reading.

At Elizabeth Estates Public Library, we are committed to partnering with schools, teachers, and families to make that vision a reality. We welcome class visits, storytelling sessions, library memberships, and literacy programmes that encourage children to discover the joy of reading.

[Slide 9 — A Book Is…]

Before I close, I would like to show you something. 

Hold up a children’s picture book.

To many people, this is simply a children’s book. But to me, it is much more.

It is a passport. 

It is a teacher. 

It is a window through which a child sees the world. 

And sometimes, it is a mirror in which a child first sees themselves.

Never underestimate the power of the book in your hands. It may be the very book that inspires the next teacher, the next doctor, the next entrepreneur, the next scientist, the next librarian, or even the next Prime Minister of the Commonwealth of The Bahamas.

So, I leave you with two thoughts. First: a library is not just a place where children learn to read; it is a place where they learn about the world, discover who they are, and begin to imagine who they can become.

And finally, remember the proverb with which I began: Every mickle mek a muckle. Every page turned, every story shared, every question answered, every child encouraged, and every visit to the library — it all adds up. Every little effort you make today is helping to build a stronger child, a stronger family, a stronger community, and ultimately, a stronger Bahamas.

Thank you for the work you do every day. Thank you for believing in our children. Thank you for partnering with our public libraries in nurturing a lifelong love of reading. May God continue to bless each of you, and may God continue to bless our beautiful Commonwealth of The Bahamas.

Thank you, and have a wonderful day.
